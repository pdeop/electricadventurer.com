<section class="ls-section">
<span class="ls-label">The Setup</span>
<h2>A Rivian owner walks into a rental lot</h2>

<p>Denver, personal business, two days in the Parker and Aurora suburbs. The rental happened to be an F-150 Lightning Standard Range. No agenda, no test plan. Just a truck.</p>

<p>I own both an R1S and an R1T. The R1T is the direct competitor here, truck to truck. Both are purpose-built EVs and have never pretended otherwise. The Lightning is something different: <strong>Ford took the best-selling vehicle in America and swapped the ICE powertrain for electric.</strong> Same truck. Same bones. Different soul. Two days wasn't enough to fully resolve that tension. But it was enough to form a real opinion.</p>
</section>

<section class="ls-section">
<span class="ls-label">Behind the Wheel</span>
<h2>It drives like it forgot it's a full-size truck</h2>

<p>The Lightning is fast and responsive in a way that doesn't match its footprint. The turning radius felt tighter than I expected, tighter than the R1T, which matters more than people think in city driving and parking lots. The suspension is soft and well-damped, more so than either Rivian, which run planted and sporty but firmer. Visibility is commanding. The frunk swallowed a full-size suitcase and two backpacks without ceremony. That's usable space, not a party trick.</p>

<div class="ls-callout">The sound system was almost on par with Gen 1 Rivian audio. Not what I expected from a rental truck. Ford put real money into the cabin.</div>

<p>The limits show in corners. Push it through a sweeping on-ramp and it reminds you it's body-on-frame: body roll, a hint of float. That's not a flaw; it's the honest physics of what this vehicle is. The R1T handles more like a sport truck. Different mandates.</p>
</section>

<section class="ls-section">
<span class="ls-label">The Hard Part</span>
<h2>The charging experience was rough, and that's the product's problem, not mine</h2>

<p>Picked up the truck at 83% SOC. No NACS adapter with me, a mistake I won't repeat. What followed was a lesson in why the charging network matters as much as the vehicle.</p>

<div class="ls-charge">
  <h4>Charging Log — Parker / Aurora Suburbs</h4>
  <div class="ls-charge-event"><span class="ls-status down">DOWN</span><span>ChargePoint station: app showed available, arrived to find it offline. No indication of the outage in-app.</span></div>
  <div class="ls-charge-event"><span class="ls-status slow">SLOW</span><span>Jolt charger near a shopping center: Level 2 only, buried in a parking lot. Not useful mid-day.</span></div>
  <div class="ls-charge-event"><span class="ls-status ok">OK</span><span>Level 2 session, about an hour. Recovered enough range. Returned the truck with ~35 miles remaining.</span></div>

  <img src="/images/lightning/lightning-3.jpg" alt="A DC fast charger screen reading UNAVAILABLE">

</div>

<p>Colorado's fast charger build-out is highway-corridor focused. The suburban surface street grid in Parker and Aurora is a different story: sparse, unreliable, and heavily L2. That's partly an infrastructure problem, not uniquely Ford's. But the Lightning makes it worse: <strong>the native nav has no meaningful DC fast charger integration. Not unreliable routing — absent routing. I couldn't get the truck to show me where to charge. No battery preconditioning either.</strong> On the Rivian, both happen quietly and automatically. On the Lightning, you're on your own.</p>

<p>Some range anxiety on day two. Not because I was in danger, but because I was flying blind in an unfamiliar truck with no intuition for its efficiency curve. A NACS adapter would have unlocked the Supercharger network and changed the whole picture. Lesson: bring one if you're renting a Lightning.</p>
</section>

<div class="ls-divider">· · ·</div>

<section class="ls-section">
<span class="ls-label">Software</span>
<h2>The infotainment is where it falls behind most visibly</h2>

<p>Some of this may be rental limitations. But even accounting for that, the infotainment feels like it was built by a team that understood trucks and understood software separately and never fully bridged the two. DC fast charger routing is absent, not just weak. Battery preconditioning doesn't exist. These aren't luxury features; on a purpose-built EV platform, they're table stakes. The Rivian handles both quietly and automatically. The Lightning asks you to figure it out yourself.</p>
</section>

<div class="ls-thesis">
  <blockquote>Ford took the F-150 and swapped the ICE powertrain for electric. Same truck. Same bones. <em>Different soul.</em> That's the uniqueness and the beauty of it, and also its ceiling.</blockquote>
  <p>The Lightning is a genuine EV achievement when you view it as what it is: the world's most popular truck, electrified without alienating the people who buy it. For someone who hauls things occasionally, commutes on open roads, and charges at home, it's a compelling choice. The fact that Ford discontinued it says more about corporate will than about the vehicle's merit.</p>
</div>

<section class="ls-section">
<span class="ls-label">Bottom Line</span>
<h2>Who is this truck for?</h2>

<p>Someone who hauls things occasionally. Someone whose commute doesn't involve crowded streets. Someone who charges at home and doesn't depend on public DC fast charging to make their day work. If that's you and you're an F-150 loyalist, the Lightning makes a genuinely compelling case.</p>

<p>It's not a Rivian and it was never trying to be. The R1T was engineered as an EV from a blank sheet of paper. The Lightning was engineered to make electrification feel familiar to people who already love the F-150. Both are legitimate mandates. The Lightning executes its mandate impressively.</p>

<p>I came out more impressed than I expected. The bones are good. The cabin is good. The frunk is legitimately useful. What's missing is the software layer that makes an EV actually livable outside your driveway. Ford discontinued the Lightning before they got there, but the underlying thesis — electrify the truck America already trusts — was always sound. Here's hoping their next electric truck platform is built from the ground up to own it.</p>
</section>
